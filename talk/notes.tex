\section{Infinite chess and open games}

\begin{definition}[Infinite chess; \EH{Section 1, pp.\,2--3}, \EHP{Section 2, p.\,4}]
Chess on $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$ with the usual pieces and moves. There is no edge, so no promotion,
castling or en passant. White wins by checkmating black. A game that never ends is a draw, and so is
stalemate. The fifty-move and threefold-repetition rules are dropped: both exist only to force finite games,
and infinite play is already a draw. A \emph{board position} assigns to each square a piece or nothing; a
\emph{position} adds whose turn it is. Positions may have infinitely many pieces; a position is
\emph{computable} if the board position is a Turing computable function of the square. A \emph{strategy}
for a player from $p$ maps each finite sequence of moves from $p$ to a next move, and is \emph{winning} if it
defeats every opposing strategy.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Open game; \EHP{Section 2, p.\,3}]
A two-player game of perfect information is \emph{open for a player} if every play won by that player is
already won after finitely many moves; equivalently, that player's winning plays form an open set in the
product topology on plays.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
Chess is open for white and for black, since checkmate is a property of one position. A draw by infinite
play is not decided at any finite stage, so the valuation below is one-sided: defined from white's point of
view and, separately, from black's. Section~3 holds for any open game.
\end{remark}

\section{Ordinals}

\begin{fact}[Standard; \EH{p.\,5}, \EHP{p.\,4}]\label{fact:ordinals}
The ordinals extend the natural numbers:
$0,1,2,\ldots,\omega,\omega+1,\ldots,\omega\cdot 2,\ldots,\omega^2,\ldots,\omega^3,\ldots,\omega^4,\ldots$
Every ordinal $\alpha$ has a successor $\alpha+1$; a nonzero ordinal with no immediate predecessor, such as
$\omega$ or $\omega^2$, is a \emph{limit}. Every ordinal below $\omega^\omega$ has a unique normal form
$\omega^k\cdot a_k+\cdots+\omega\cdot a_1+a_0$ with natural coefficients, and two are compared at the first
coefficient where they differ, from the left. The ordinals are \emph{well-ordered}: every nonempty set has a
least element, so there is no infinite strictly decreasing sequence $\alpha_0>\alpha_1>\cdots$. A countable
set of countable ordinals has a countable supremum.
\stated
\end{fact}

\section{Game values}

\begin{definition}[Game value; \EH{Definition 1, pp.\,4--5}, \EHP{Section 2, p.\,3}]\label{def:value}
Fix white as the designated player. Values are assigned to positions by recursion on $\alpha$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item A position in which white has already won has value $0$.
  \item If $p$ has white to move, $p$ has value $\alpha+1$ if and only if $\alpha$ is least such that some
    legal white move from $p$ reaches a position of value $\alpha$.
  \item If $p$ has black to move and black has a legal move, $p$ has a value if and only if every legal black
    move from $p$ reaches a position with a value, and then the value of $p$ is the supremum of those values.
\end{enumerate}
The valuation is the least assignment obeying (i) to (iii). A position with no value is \emph{unvalued}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:value}
(a) At white's turn one good move suffices; at black's turn every move must be covered.
(b) By (ii) a white-to-move position has a successor value. So a position of limit value $\lambda$ has black
to move, and every black move drops strictly below $\lambda$.
(c) Finite value $n$ means mate in $n$ for white with $n$ optimal \EH{p.\,5}.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[The fundamental observation; \EH{p.\,5}, \EHP{pp.\,3--4}]\label{thm:fundamental}
If a position has a value, white has a winning strategy from it. If a position has no value, black has a
strategy that prevents white from ever winning.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $p$ has a value. White plays the \emph{value-reducing strategy}: at each turn, move to a position of
least value, which by (ii) is one less than the current value. By (iii) a black move never raises the value and
never reaches an unvalued position. So if $v_k$ is the value at white's $k$th turn, white moves to $v_k-1$
and black's reply has value at most $v_k-1$, whence $v_0>v_1>v_2>\cdots$. By Fact~\ref{fact:ordinals} the
sequence is finite, so the play reaches value $0$, a win for white. (In a valued position black always has a
legal move or white has already won, by (iii), so play never stalls.)

Suppose $p$ is unvalued. If it is white's turn, no white move reaches a valued position, since by (ii) such a
move would give $p$ a value. If it is black's turn and black has a legal move, some black move reaches an
unvalued position, since otherwise (iii) would value $p$; if black has no legal move the position is
stalemate. Black plays the \emph{value-maintaining strategy}, always choosing such a move. Every position of
the play is unvalued, in particular never of value $0$, so white never wins.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[\EHP{p.\,4}]
In chess a position is a forced win for white exactly when it has a value for white, a forced win for black
exactly when it has a value for black, and otherwise both players can force a draw. In an open game without
draws, preventing white from winning is winning for black, so Theorem~\ref{thm:fundamental} is the
Gale--Stewart theorem that open games are determined [GS53].
\end{corollary}

\begin{example}[Counting down from $\alpha$; \EH{p.\,6}]\label{ex:countdown}
Black plays a strictly descending sequence of ordinals below $\alpha$, white says ``OK, fine'' after each,
and white wins when black plays $0$. The position ``black to move at $\beta$'' has value exactly $\beta$: at
$0$ white has won; at $\beta>0$ each black move to $\gamma<\beta$ leads to a white-to-move position whose only
continuation is ``black to move at $\gamma$'', of value $\gamma+1$ by induction, and
$\sup_{\gamma<\beta}(\gamma+1)=\beta$. The countdown game on our slides is our own variant: a counter shows
an ordinal, white steps a successor down by one, black chooses any smaller ordinal at a limit. The same
induction gives it value $\alpha$.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Values as announcements; \EH{pp.\,5--7}, \EHP{pp.\,4--5}]\label{prop:announce}
Play from value $\alpha$ is a countdown from $\alpha$: white lowers the value by exactly one at each of his
moves, and black must choose a smaller ordinal at each limit. With black to move, a value of at least
$\omega$ lets black \emph{announce} a number $n$, a lower bound on the length of the remaining play;
$\omega\cdot n$, make $n$ announcements in turn; $\omega^2$, make a \emph{large announcement}, the number of
announcements to follow; $\omega^2\cdot m$, make $m$ large announcements; $\omega^3$, make a \emph{huge
announcement}, the number of large announcements to follow; $\omega^4$, make a \emph{meta-announcement}, the
number of huge announcements. From $\omega^4$ black might play to
$\omega^3\cdot 5+\omega^2\cdot 17+\omega\cdot 34+1234$: a meta-announcement of $6$, a huge announcement of
$18$, a large announcement of $35$, and $1234$ moves before the next announcement \EHP{p.\,5}.
\end{proposition}

\section{Positions of value \texorpdfstring{$\omega$, $\omega^2$ and $\omega^3$}{omega, omega squared and omega cubed}}

\begin{proposition}[\EH{Observation 2, p.\,7}]
For each $n$ some position with four pieces (white king, queen and rook, black king) has value $n$: white
rolls the black king along a rank with alternating checks. No three-piece position has value above $5$.
\stated
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[A finite position with value $\omega$; \EH{Figure 3, pp.\,8--9}]\label{prop:omega}
The position of Figure~3, black to move, has value $\omega$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{sketch}
Black lifts the e-rook to any height; white rolls the black king up the e-file with alternating rook and
queen checks until it is mated against the rook: 1\ldots Re10 2.Rf5+ Ke6 3.Qd5+ Ke7 4.Rf7+ Ke8 5.Qd7+ Ke9
6.Rf9\#. The higher the rook, the longer the roll, so after black's first move the values are finite and
unbounded, with supremum $\omega$. Every other black first move is mated in at most three (1\ldots Rf6 2.Re4+ Kf5
3.Qd5\#, 1\ldots Rd6 2.Qf5\#, and so on). White has nothing faster: skewering the lifted rook with 2.Re4+
takes the white rook off the f-file and frees the black king from the net, after which black sacrifices his
pieces and rook and queen cannot mate a bare king. The paper gives these lines and says ``we claim''; no
exhaustive check is written out.
\end{sketch}

\begin{proposition}[Infinite positions with value $\omega$ and $\omega^2$; \EH{Figures 5--7, pp.\,12--15}]
\label{prop:omega2}
(a) In the door positions of Figure~5 an attacked black rook lifts to height $n$ and is captured, opening a
hole in an infinite pawn column; white must advance the pawns below the hole $n$ times before the mating
material is released. Value $\omega$.
(b) In Figure~7 a pawn column divides the plane; the emphasised pawn below the keystone bishop is a
portcullis, and once it moves the queens on the left flood the right half and mate. Black lifts the centre
rook to height $n$ and white must advance the column $n$ times to free the bishop; after each advance black's
free rook on the right checks the white king from a distance $k$ of black's choosing, and white, who cannot
allow perpetual check, walks toward the rook for $k$ moves before black retreats. Black announces $n$, then
$k$ in each of $n$ rounds: value $\omega^2$. For the upper bound the paper argues that each deviation mates
sooner: capturing a wall pawn opens the hole early, and sacrificing the rook forfeits the harassment and
leaves value $\omega$.
\stated
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Value $\omega^2\cdot k$ and $\omega^3$; \EH{Figures 8--10, pp.\,15--20}]\label{prop:omega3}
(a) A \emph{locked door with key} (Figure~8) is a rook tower whose rook need not move until a white
\emph{key} pawn attacks the black \emph{lock} pawn guarding it; black lets the key capture the lock, the rook
is attacked and lifts, and the pawn advances that follow release the next key pawn. Chaining $k$ towers
(Figure~9, $k=4$) gives value $\omega^2\cdot k$: $k$ large announcements, each followed by the play of
Proposition~\ref{prop:omega2}(b).
(b) In Figure~10 the attacked bishop at m6 runs up the diagonal past as many towers as black chooses before
it is captured; the capture releases the first key pawn, and the towers are consumed from right to left with
a harassment campaign after every pawn advance. Past $k$ towers the value is at least $\omega^2\cdot k$ with
$k$ unbounded, so the position has value $\omega^3$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{sketch}
For the upper bound: without the bishop the position is drawn, so white must activate towers in order and
black gains nothing by activating one himself; if black leaves the bishop, 2.Bl5 frees the k5 pawn and mates
quickly; and irrelevant rook moves in towers beyond the active one cannot all be answered, since infinite play
would draw, but white answers all except, say, every tenth, never one in an adjacent tower, and an ignored
rook can be trapped on its third move (Figure~11), so black gains a bounded amount while giving up a
harassment campaign worth $\omega$. The paper indicates $\omega^3\cdot 2$ by prefixing a second $\omega^3$
block that releases the key bishop (p.\,20).
\end{sketch}

\section{The position with value \texorpdfstring{$\omega^4$}{omega to the fourth}}

\begin{theorem}[\EHP{Theorem 1, p.\,3}]\label{thm:omega4}
There is a position in infinite chess with game value $\omega^4$. The position is computable and involves
infinitely many pieces.
\end{theorem}

The previous records were $\omega^3$ and $\omega^3\cdot 4$ \EHP{p.\,1}. The proof is the construction and
analysis of 5.2 to 5.8, carried out in prose in the paper; we mark it a sketch throughout.

\begin{construction}[The position; \EHP{Figure 1, pp.\,5--7}]\label{con:position}
Black to move. Three components: the \emph{rook tower block} in the northeast, adapted from Figure~10 of
[EH14]; the \emph{throne room} in the northwest, holding both kings; and the \emph{bishop-cannon battery}
in the south, cannons in the southeast, gateway terminal wings in the southwest, an enclosed arena between.
There are infinitely many towers, one cannon of each finite size and one wing of each finite size. In [EH14,
Figure~10] the tower block alone had value $\omega^2$ and the harassment arena multiplied the delay by
$\omega$, giving $\omega^3=\omega\cdot\omega^2$; here the battery multiplies by $\omega^2$, giving
$\omega^4=\omega^2\cdot\omega^2$ \EHP{p.\,7}.
\end{construction}

\begin{lemma}[Tempo in the throne room; \EHP{Figure 2, pp.\,7--8}]\label{lem:tempo}
No piece in the throne room can move. A white bishop reaching the marked diagonal mates at once
(1.Bc10\#). A black bishop reaching its entry diagonal needs exactly three further moves
(1\ldots Bc11 2\ldots Bg7 3\ldots Bj10 4\ldots Bh12\#). Hence if a black bishop reaches its diagonal and a
white bishop reaches the white diagonal two moves later, white mates first.
\end{lemma}

\begin{sketch}
Inspection of Figure~2. We have checked the two lines, not the absence of a faster black mate.
\end{sketch}

\begin{construction}[The rook towers; \EHP{Figure 3, pp.\,8--10}]\label{con:towers}
The black bishop at f7 is attacked, and its capture or any exchange releases a white bishop that mates
quickly, so black's first move sends it up the diagonal, say 1\ldots Bq18. White captures with a pawn and
uses that pawn to pierce the upper pawn diagonal, releasing bishops that let the key pawn o21 advance against
the guard pawn n24. Black does not capture, which would free the next tower sooner, so the key takes the
guard and attacks the rook m25, which lifts to a height of black's choosing and is captured from the west.
This opens a hole in the l-file pawn column, which white walks down one pawn move at a time until the bishop
m24 can enter it; the bishops then clear the way for the key pawn l18 to attack the next guard pawn k21, and
the process repeats one tower west. In the last tower the pawn c16 releases the mating bishop d15. Each
tower costs white a number $h$ of moves fixed by the rook lift, during which black is free to play elsewhere.
Sending the first bishop past $n$ towers gives a position of value in $[\omega^3\cdot n,\,\omega^3\cdot(n+1))$
\EHP{p.\,9}.
\end{construction}

\begin{lemma}[Gateway wings; \EHP{Figure 4, pp.\,11--12}]\label{lem:wing}
A wing of length $n$ is a diagonal hallway with $n$ gates, each a door pawn, one attack square on the hallway,
and a white guard bishop in a tower behind it. A black bishop on the wing's entrance diagonal can make $n+1$
forced-reply threats in succession: each attack square once, in any order, then the capture of a door
(1\ldots Bp8 2.Bq12 Bj14 3.Bk18 Bd20 4.Be24 Bxf22 5.Bd23).
\end{lemma}

\begin{sketch}
If white ignores an attack, black takes the door, exits, and reaches the throne-room diagonal three moves
ahead of the guard, which mates by Lemma~\ref{lem:tempo}. If white steps the guard one square toward the
gate, the guard can follow an exiting bishop along a parallel path two moves behind, and by
Lemma~\ref{lem:tempo} white mates first, so black does not exit. Each reply is one guard step and touches
nothing else.
\end{sketch}

\begin{lemma}[Bishop cannons; \EHP{Figure 5, pp.\,12--14}]\label{lem:cannon}
A cannon of size $b$ is a stack of black bishops behind a wall, $b$ of them capped by a black pawn. Black
\emph{opens} it by moving the front bishop along the exit diagonal to the entrance diagonal of an unused
wing; this is not a threat, and white gets one free move. Every capped bishop then \emph{fires} with force:
its departure uncovers a pawn advance against the wall, and white must advance the matching guard pawn to
shore the wall up. Each fired bishop lands facing an unused wing of any size $g$ and spends $g+1$ forced
replies there by Lemma~\ref{lem:wing}. So opening a cannon of size $b$ buys forced play of order
$\omega\cdot b$, and the battery is reusable, since only finitely many cannons and wings are ever used.
\end{lemma}

\begin{sketch}
After the h8 bishop fires: 2.j3 h8 3.j4 h7 4.j5 hxi6 5.jxi6, meeting the threat of i5 and i6 opening the
chain for the bishop on g9; capturing the attacking pawn instead lets i7 advance and open a hole.
\end{sketch}

\begin{proposition}[The value along the main line; \EHP{pp.\,14--16}]\label{prop:formula}
In the main line white works through the towers as in Construction~\ref{con:towers}, and at every free
black move black opens a fresh cannon, as large as he likes, then plays out all its forced replies. White's
only chances to progress in a tower are the free move after each opening, the first move, and the moment each
rook is attacked. To a main-line position, outside the last four or five moves when the battery's threats are
too slow to carry force, associate
\[
  \omega^3\cdot r+\omega^2\cdot h+\omega\cdot b+g,
\]
with $r$ the towers not yet activated, $h$ the white moves left in the current tower up to the attack on the
next rook, $b$ the capped bishops left in the active cannon, and $g$ the forced-reply threats left to the
current bishop in its wing. A gateway attack lowers $g$ by one. At $g=0$ a shot lowers $b$ by one and makes
$g$ as large as black likes. At $b=0$ opening a cannon lets white lower $h$ by one and then makes $b$ as large
as black likes. At $h=0$ the next tower activates, $r$ falls by one and the rook lift sets $h$. So the main
line is a countdown from $\omega^4$ in the sense of Proposition~\ref{prop:announce}, and if neither side
gains by deviating these ordinals are the game values.
\end{proposition}

\begin{sketch}
The transitions are read off Construction~\ref{con:towers} and Lemmas~\ref{lem:wing} and
\ref{lem:cannon}; the deviations are Proposition~\ref{prop:deviations}.
\end{sketch}

\begin{proposition}[Deviations; \EHP{pp.\,16--20}]\label{prop:deviations}
Neither player gains by leaving the main line: white has no quicker mate, and black can neither win nor
impose a larger value.
\end{proposition}

\begin{sketch}
Black. Extra bishops freed into the arena are penned in by the black-square pawn wall and can only attack
door pawns, each answered by a guard step. Bishops waiting in captured doorways are harmless: the first to
exit lets the guard out, and black-square bishops cannot block the white-square mating path. Reordering the
battery does not help: with $b$ the capped bishops in all open cannons and $g$ the longest run of forced
replies available, any move that is not a forced reply hands white a reduction of $h$, worth more than
anything it gains. Rook moves in towers beyond the active one are handled as in [EH14, Figure~11]: white
ignores a fixed proportion, never the same rook twice nor an adjacent tower, and lowers $h$ faster than if
black had fired the battery; a rook moved west of the active tower is captured so as to activate that tower.
Exiting a gateway with the guard two behind loses by Lemma~\ref{lem:tempo}. 1\ldots Rxd15 is met by
2.exd15 freeing the bishop f15; and the black pawn freed by white's first capture must stay put, since
descending it opens the lower wall, which is why that wall is doubled here, unlike in [EH14].

White. The only white bishops in the south are the guards, confined to four squares each; the bishops freed
in the towers sit above the pawn diagonal with no way out, and opening further towers only frees more of them.
Guarding before a threat is useless, since black has infinitely many unused cannons and wings. Breaking the
cannon wall with a guard pawn lets a black bishop out to mate.
\end{sketch}

\begin{remark}[The compact battery; \EHP{Section 4, pp.\,20--21}]\label{rem:compact}
The cannons can be placed with their back ends on a vertical eastern wall, still one of each size, and
every fired bishop can still attack arbitrarily large wings. The value stays $\omega^4$ and the southeast
quadrant is empty. The authors propose using it for $\omega^5$, with a further battery whose shots initiate
the present cannons with force, but give no position.
\end{remark}

\section{Bounds, the conjecture and open problems}

\begin{definition}[The omega one of chess; \EH{Definition 3, p.\,10}]\label{def:omegaone}
$\omega_1^{\Ch}$ is the supremum of the values of the winning finite positions for white;
$\bomega_1^{\Ch}$, the boldface version, the supremum over all winning positions, infinitely many pieces
allowed;
$\omega_1^{\Ch,c}$ the supremum over computable positions, and likewise for other complexity classes;
$\omega_1^{\Ch_3}$ and $\bomega_1^{\Ch_3}$ the analogues for infinite three-dimensional chess.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[\EH{Theorem 4, pp.\,10--12}]\label{thm:bounds}
(1) $\omega_1^{\Ch}\le\omega_1^{\Ch,c}\le\omega_1^{\Ch,\mathrm{hyp}}\le\omega_1^{\CK}$, the Church--Kleene
ordinal, the supremum of the computable ordinals; so the value of a winning finite, computable or
hyperarithmetic position is a computable ordinal, and a player with a winning strategy from $p$ has one
hyperarithmetic in $p$. (2) $\bomega_1^{\Ch}\le\omega_1$; the value of a winning position $p$ is a
$p$-computable ordinal. (3) The same bounds hold in three dimensions.
\stated
\end{theorem}

For (2): each player has only countably many legal moves from any position, so every supremum in
Definition~\ref{def:value} is countable by Fact~\ref{fact:ordinals}, and $\omega_1$ is never reached. For
(1) the paper shows that ``$p$ has value $\alpha$'' is $\Sigma_1$ over admissible sets and applies
$\Sigma_1$-boundedness in $L_{\omega_1^{\CK}}$.

\begin{conjecture}[\EH{Conjecture 5, p.\,21}]
$\omega_1^{\Ch}=\omega_1^{\Ch,c}=\omega_1^{\Ch,\mathrm{hyp}}=\omega_1^{\CK}$ and $\bomega_1^{\Ch}=\omega_1$.
The best finite lower bound known is $\omega$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:omega}); answers to Wästlund's
MathOverflow question reach $\omega\cdot n$. The computable infinite positions give
$\omega^3<\omega_1^{\Ch,c}$ [EH14] and $\omega^4<\omega_1^{\Ch,c}$ [EHP17].
\end{conjecture}

\begin{theorem}[\EH{Theorem 10, p.\,28; Corollary 12, p.\,35}]
Every countable ordinal is the value of a position in infinite three-dimensional chess, so
$\bomega_1^{\Ch_3}=\omega_1$; every computable ordinal is the value of a computable position, so
$\omega_1^{\Ch_3,c}=\omega_1^{\CK}$.
\stated
\end{theorem}

The idea: for a well-founded tree $T\subseteq\omega^{<\omega}$, the game in which black climbs through $T$
and loses on reaching a leaf has value equal to the rank of $T$, since the two recursions coincide
(Lemma~11); every countable ordinal is such a rank; and the paper embeds this game in three-dimensional chess
with kings, pawns and bishops.

\paragraph{Open problems.}
(1) Is there a finite position with value $\omega^2$? None is known; [EH14, p.\,21] suggests an inefficient
mating net that black can push far away. (2) Is $\omega^4$ the largest value in two dimensions? The
$\omega^5$ idea of Remark~\ref{rem:compact} has no position behind it. (3) Is $\bomega_1^{\Ch}=\omega_1$
in two dimensions? The authors tried to embed trees as in Theorem~6.4 and found the plane too cramped
(p.\,21).

\section*{Glossary}

\begin{multicols}{2}\footnotesize
\begin{description}[style=unboxed,leftmargin=0pt]
  \item[Announcement] Black's move at a limit stage, choosing a smaller ordinal; large, huge and meta
    announcements are the choices at $\omega^2$, $\omega^3$ and $\omega^4$.
  \item[Forced reply] A black move white must answer at once or be mated.
  \item[Door, portcullis] A pawn whose advance releases white's mating material.
  \item[Key pawn, lock pawn] The white pawn whose advance activates a tower, and the black pawn guarding it.
  \item[Rook tower] One unit of white's main attack: guard pawn, rook, pawn column, bishops.
  \item[Throne room] The locked chamber holding both kings in the $\omega^4$ position.
  \item[Gateway terminal wing] A diagonal hallway with gates, each a door pawn, an attack square and a guard.
  \item[Bishop cannon] A stack of pawn-capped bishops; opening it is free, each shot is a forced reply.
  \item[$r$, $h$, $b$, $g$] Towers left, white moves left in the current tower, shots left in the active
    cannon, forced replies left in the active wing.
  \item[Church--Kleene ordinal $\omega_1^{\CK}$] The least non-computable ordinal, the supremum of the
    computable ones.
\end{description}
\end{multicols}
